How much does a lumper service cost?

The short answer

Lumper work is priced three ways: a flat fee per trailer, a rate per case handled, or an hourly rate for the crew. As market context — not a quote — industry reporting commonly places typical lumper fees around $150–$300+ per load, with hand-stacked grocery loads running higher. What a specific load costs depends on the freight: floor-loaded, mixed and shifted loads take more labor than clean palletized ones.

The three pricing models

1. Per-trailer flat fee

One price per load, usually set by freight type: a palletized load at one rate, a floor-loaded load at a higher one. This is the most common model at the dock because it is simple, predictable for the facility, and easy for a driver or carrier to document for reimbursement.

2. Per-case (per-piece) rate

The charge follows the number of cases the crew handles. Grocery and retail distribution favor this model because volume varies enormously load to load. It rewards accurate counting — which is why per-case operations live and die by their door counts and logs.

3. Hourly crew rate

The customer buys the crew's time. Hourly fits work whose scope can't be known until the doors open: restack and recovery projects, container unloading, repack jobs, and multi-trailer surge coverage where the operator manages the crew against the clock.

Market context — not a quote

Trade-press and carrier reporting on lumper fees commonly describes typical charges in the range of $150–$300+ per load, with grocery hand-stack loads — thousands of cases moved entirely by hand — running higher still. Fees vary by region, facility and freight, and any specific load requires its own quote. These figures describe the market a lumper service operates in; they are not any one company's price list.

What drives the price of a load

  • Floor-loaded vs. palletized. The single biggest factor. A palletized trailer can be off in a fraction of the time; a floor-loaded trailer is moved case by case, by hand.
  • Freight type and case weight. Heavy, awkward or fragile freight slows the crew and may require more hands per door.
  • Sorting complexity. A single-SKU load comes off in streams. A mixed load that must be sorted by purchase order or aisle takes counting and staging labor on top of unloading.
  • Restack and repack work. Freight that shifted in transit has to be rebuilt into stable pallets and re-wrapped before the receiver will take it — skilled work, billed accordingly.
  • Breakdown requirements. Some receivers require pallets broken down to specific heights or configurations, adding a second handling pass.
  • Schedule pressure. Tight appointment windows, after-hours arrivals and surge coverage price in the cost of having a crew on call.
  • Volume and standing arrangements. A facility or carrier with a standing schedule gives the operator predictable work — and predictable work is priced better than one-off emergencies.

Who pays — and why the paperwork is part of the price

Many warehouses contract a lumper service directly. At other facilities the fee lands on the carrier, and for years drivers paid it out of pocket at the dock and fought for reimbursement afterward — often losing the argument over a missing receipt or a disputed count. A professionally run lumper operation treats the documents as part of the product: the seal photographed before it breaks, the count taken at the door, and a per-trailer unload log signed by the crew lead. That packet is what a reimbursement claim, and any shortage dispute, is settled with.

How operators price their own crews

On the operator's side of the dock, pricing starts with labor math: every position on the crew carries a bill rate (what the client is charged per hour) and a pay rate (what the worker earns per hour), and the margin between them has to cover leads, insurance, equipment and no-show backfill. Frequently asked questions

How much does a lumper service cost per load?

Market reporting on lumper fees commonly places a typical unloading charge around $150 to $300 or more per load, with hand-stacked grocery loads running higher. That is market context, not a quote: the actual price of a load depends on the freight, whether it is floor-loaded or palletized, and how much restack and sorting work the trailer needs.

How is lumper pricing usually structured?

Three models dominate. A per-trailer flat fee charges one price per load based on freight type. A per-case or per-piece rate charges by the number of cases handled, which suits high-volume grocery and retail freight. An hourly crew rate bills the crew's time, which suits restack projects, container work and jobs where the scope is hard to predict.

What makes one trailer cost more to unload than another?

Floor-loaded freight costs more than palletized freight because every case is moved by hand. Heavy cases, mixed SKUs that must be sorted, freight that shifted and needs restacking, breakdown and repack work, and tight appointment windows all add labor. A clean, palletized, single-SKU trailer is the cheapest load on the dock.

Who pays the lumper fee — the warehouse, the carrier, or the driver?

All three arrangements exist. Many facilities contract and pay a lumper service directly. At others, the fee is charged to the carrier, and drivers historically paid it out of pocket at the dock and sought reimbursement — which is why a receipt and a signed per-trailer unload log matter: they are the documents a reimbursement claim and a count dispute are settled with.
