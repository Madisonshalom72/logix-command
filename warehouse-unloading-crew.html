Staffing a warehouse unloading crew: overflow, peak & no-show coverage

The short answer

The reliable pattern is a core in-house receiving team plus a contracted lumper service for everything that doesn't fit the plan: peak surges, floor-loaded trailers, and no-show days. The lumper crew works to your pallet and staging specs — and every trailer it touches produces a verified seal, a door count, and a signed per-trailer unload log.

The coverage problem, honestly stated

Inbound labor demand is spiky in ways a fixed roster can't absorb. A floor-loaded trailer can take several times the labor of a palletized one. Peak season multiplies doors. A single no-show on a thin crew turns into detention charges, driver frustration and a dock that starts the next shift already behind. Hiring for the peak means paying for idle hands in the valley; staffing for the valley means drowning at the peak. That gap is exactly what a lumper service exists to fill.

What a managed unloading crew does at your dock

  • Checks in and takes the door assignment — one crew lead as your single point of contact; one voice controls each trailer door.
  • Verifies the seal before it breaks — seal number read against the paperwork, photographed with the trailer number in frame; the broken seal is kept.
  • Counts at the door — cases counted as freight comes off, against the bill of lading, with damage, overage or shortage reported immediately, not at shift's end.
  • Builds to your spec — restack, breakdown and pallet configuration to your Ti/Hi and staging requirements, wrapped and labeled for put-away.
  • Signs the log — trailer number, seal number, start/stop times and count, signed by the lead before the trailer is released.

The paperwork is the differentiator

Any labor source can move freight. The reason facilities renew managed lumper contracts is the record: a verified seal and a signed door count on every trailer. When a shortage claim lands — shipper blames carrier, carrier blames receiver — the facility holding a per-trailer log with a photographed seal and a lead's signature is the one holding the facts. In LOGIX COMMAND, that log isn't an afterthought form; it is generated as part of the job's paperwork packet before the crew is ever dispatched, and the job can't close out complete without it.

How to engage a lumper service

  1. Walk the dock together. A short site survey: doors, dock equipment, pallet specs, appointment system, safety rules, where the facility's responsibility ends and the crew's begins.
  2. Agree the pricing model. Per-trailer, per-case or hourly crew — matched to your freight mix. (See our lumper cost guide for how the models work and what market fees look like.)
  3. Run a pilot trailer. One load, full process — seal, count, log — so the paperwork packet is in your hand the same day and can be judged on evidence.
  4. Set the standing arrangement. Scheduled doors, surge triggers, and no-show backfill terms, so coverage is a phone call, not a negotiation, at 6 a.m.

Five questions to ask any lumper provider

  • Can I see a current certificate of insurance?
  • How are your crews trained — dock safety, trailer entry, seal and count discipline?
  • Who leads the crew on my dock, by name?
  • What document does each trailer produce, and can I see a sample?
  • When one of your people doesn't show, what happens — specifically?

Frequently asked questions

How do warehouses cover unloading when inbound volume spikes?

Most facilities combine a core in-house receiving team with a contracted lumper service for the overflow: peak-season surges, floor-loaded trailers that take far more labor than palletized ones, and no-show coverage when their own crew or a scheduled service falls short. The lumper crew works to the facility's pallet and staging specs and documents each trailer it touches.

What paperwork should an unloading crew produce?

At minimum, a per-trailer unload log: the trailer number, the seal number verified against the paperwork before the seal is broken, start and finish times, the case count taken at the door, and any damage, overage or shortage noted, signed by the crew lead. That log is what settles lumper-fee reimbursements and shortage disputes between shipper, carrier and receiver.

How do verified counts reduce shortage claims?

A shortage claim is usually an argument about what was in the trailer when the doors opened. A seal photographed and verified before breaking proves the load arrived intact, and a count taken at the door — before freight disperses into the warehouse — fixes the number at the only moment it can be known. Signed documentation converts a three-way dispute into a record.

What should a DC manager ask a lumper service before the first trailer?

Ask for a current certificate of insurance, how their crews are trained (dock safety, seal and count discipline), who leads the crew on site, what documentation each trailer produces, and how they backfill a crew member who does not show. A managed operation answers all five with documents and named leads, not assurances.
